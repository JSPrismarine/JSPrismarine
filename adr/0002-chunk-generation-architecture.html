Generate a chunk through a status ladder, not a 3 by 3 region ​

Status: accepted

DataDrivenGenerator.generateChunk builds a 3 by 3 region, generates terrain for nine chunks, decorates the center, extracts one chunk and serializes the whole body behind one promise, at 1572 ms per chunk. We replace it with Java's model: a chunk exists at a named status, a holder owns one promise per status, a step table declares what each status may read and write, and a scheduler advances statuses in layers. Every status becomes a pure function of the world seed, the chunk position and the frozen outputs of the statuses below it, which is what lets a worker pool run them and what makes the output independent of generation order and worker count.

The implementation is sliced in docs/plans/chunk-generation-rewrite.md.

What we build ​

Six parts, in the order they depend on each other.

A status ladder. All ten of Java's statuses with Java's indices and parents: empty, structure_starts, structure_references, biomes, terrain, features, initialize_light, light, spawn, full. The three in the middle carry pass-through bodies for now, because lighting lives in packages/lighting, spawning in packages/spawning, and neither is sequenced against generation today. Keeping them in the ladder rather than collapsing it costs one ring of accumulated radius and buys an exact match with Java's dependency tables, so the step table is a transcription rather than a derivation, and attaching lighting later is wiring a body rather than reshaping the pyramid.

A step table. Per status, the status each neighbor at Chebyshev distance d is guaranteed to have reached, and the radius the status may write blocks into. The numbers are Java's, read out of ChunkPyramid.GENERATION_PYRAMID (java-chunk-generation-pipeline.md section 2). terrain writes radius 0; features writes radius 1; everything else writes nowhere.

A holder per chunk. One promise per status. The first caller to ask for a status creates the promise, every later caller awaits the same one. That is Java's acquireStatusBump without the compare-and-exchange, because the Node event loop makes the map insert atomic. The invariant that a (chunk, status) body runs exactly once is therefore free, and it holds whether the body runs inline or in a worker, because only the owning thread touches the holder.

A proto chunk. The intermediate state a neighbor can be handed partway through: a block palette and a Uint16Array of indices, the four heightmaps, the biome palette, and the block entity and entity spawn requests. Flat typed arrays throughout, so the whole thing moves between threads in a transfer list for 18 microseconds rather than being cloned.

A feature patch, merged in a fixed order. A chunk's feature pass reads its own live proto chunk and the terrain-status snapshot of its eight neighbors, and every write that lands outside its own chunk is recorded as a patch rather than applied to the neighbor. A chunk's final blocks are its terrain, then its own feature writes, then the eight inbound patches applied in a fixed neighbor order. A chunk may only reach full once it and all eight neighbors have passed features, which is Java's rule and is what makes a tree whole across a border.

One exception to the inbound order: a block a structure piece wrote is kept against an inbound patch. A neighbor decorated against this chunk's bare terrain and never saw the pieces, so its ore vein or grass patch was decided against stone or air that is a fortress wall or a library in the finished chunk. Java reaches the same outcome in either decoration order, since a feature tests the structure's blocks when it runs later and the piece writes over the feature when it runs earlier, so the patch carries the structure's positions and the merge drops inbound writes there.

A worker pool. piscina, six workers, each holding one seed's compiled pipeline, fed the registry documents as one SharedArrayBuffer of UTF-8 JSON. Workers run status bodies and return transferable proto chunks. The main thread owns the holders, the scheduler, the patch merge and the translation from Java states to Bedrock runtime ids.

The 454 ms is the 3 by 3 window, and it is work the design does nine times. generateChunk builds nine independent radius-0 terrain regions and merges their centres into one radius-1 region with copyCenterFrom (DataDrivenGenerator.ts:236). Walking along a row, each new chunk needs three new tiles. The terrainChunks cache holds 128 of them and then evicts, so a preload at preload-radius: 5 is 121 chunks and sits right at the edge.

The 875 ms is not what it looks like, and the second measurement pass changed this decision. It is not duplicated work, it is not a cache miss, and it is not noise evaluation. The start cache already works: startsFor is called four times per chunk in steady state rather than ten, and essentially every startAt inside those calls hits the cache. What the 875 ms is, measured over a 40-chunk walk at seed 1234, is first-time jigsaw assembly of candidate starts, at 0.83 new layouts per chunk costing about 375 ms each.

Two numbers from that pass matter more than the 875 itself.

91% of candidates are assembled and then thrown away. Over a 102 by 102 chunk area, 256 candidate starts were laid out and 234 were rejected, and those 234 consumed 87% of all layout time. The rejection is javaStructureStarts.ts:225, the biome check at the generation point, which runs after the builder at :213 has fully assembled the structure. A rejected village costs 3083 ms, a rejected trial chamber 2145 ms, a rejected desert pyramid 796 ms. The cost is jigsaw assembly, not terrain sampling: jigsawBlocksOf in packages/structures/src/jigsaw.ts:329 is 20.9% self time on its own, rescanning every block of a template inside a loop over parent jigsaws, candidate elements and rotations, with no cache.

875 ms per chunk is a 1-D artifact, not a steady-state constant. Layout arrives in bursts, one per candidate entering the window, so the eight-chunk sample that produced 875 happened to cross a couple of jigsaw candidates. The same walk over 40 chunks reads 311 ms per chunk. Filling a 102 by 102 area reads 9.4 ms per chunk, because a 1-D front drags 19 fresh chunks into the window at every step where a 2-D fill drags one. Java pays the same total, since its radius-8 structure_references requires that neighborhood to have reached structure_starts too.

The structure-level column sampling is badly shaped and is not the bottleneck. One StructureLevel.baseHeight call samples an entire 384-block column top to bottom with aquifers on and materializes it as a 384-entry array (javaStructureLevel.ts:64), where Java's getBaseHeight walks cells downward with aquifers off and stops at the first match. A desert pyramid asks for 441 columns, a shipwreck 253, a village 180. Measured, that is about 50 column misses per chunk, 6% of the chunk's density samples and 16% of layout wall time. Worth fixing, not worth leading with.

A CPU profile of the terrain pass agrees about where its own time goes: 55% of samples are inside density function and Perlin noise evaluation in @jsprismarine/worldgen, with gradDot at 16.1% and sampleAndLerp at 11.4%.

One structure cost is not in the structure code at all. createBeardifier (DataDrivenGenerator.ts:327) is the right operand of the top-level add in the overworld's final_density, so once it is installed every density sample runs it, and the closure at javaStructureBeard.ts:108 loops over every nearby piece. Measured at 98304 calls, which is one chunk, it costs 0.5 ms with no pieces, 32.5 ms with 40, 125 ms with 76 under beard_thin and 220 ms with 76 under encapsulate, which is what an ancient city asks for. Today that is paid once per terrain tile and a chunk builds three new tiles in steady state, so a chunk inside an ancient city's beard spends roughly 660 ms there. A structures-on and structures-off measurement charges that to structures; it is spent inside buildTerrain.

A warning about all of these numbers. The end-to-end ladder in worldgen-defects.md section 6 was taken on a host under heavy load, and a repeat attempt read the same chunk at 1064 ms, 8347 ms and 21435 ms. The microbenchmarks and the counts above are sound because they are relative and in-process. The wall-clock totals are not, and they should be retaken on an idle machine before anyone optimizes against them.

What each stage buys ​

The target is 50 to 100 chunks per second on an Apple M1 Pro, 6 performance cores plus 2 efficiency cores. Worker scaling on that machine peaks at 4.7 times one thread at five or six workers, confirmed twice: a synthetic Perlin kernel peaked at 4.67 to 4.84 and real deepslate chunk generation at 4.4 (parallel-chunk-generation-in-node.md section 6). So 50 chunks/s is 20 ms aggregate per chunk and 94 ms per chunk per worker, and 100 chunks/s is 10 ms aggregate and 47 ms per worker.

Two cost models, because structure layout differs by a factor of 33 between them. A fill is a square region generated at once, which is what a preload does. A front is a player walking in one direction, where every step drags a fresh column of chunks into the structure window.

Read the rows as follows.

The ladder buys 3 to 6 times, and it is not optional. It removes the 454 ms of duplicate neighbor terrain outright, because each chunk's terrain is computed once into its holder and read by up to nine feature passes. It computes each chunk's structure reference set once rather than the four times startsFor is called for it today, which is worth about 8 ms. It does not make one terrain tile or one structure layout any faster, and it does not reduce the total structure layout work at all; it only moves when that work is paid, which is also true of Java. What the ladder is really for is correctness and parallelism. It is the only shape in which every status is a pure function, which is the precondition for everything below it.

Rejecting a structure before assembling it is the whole structure problem. 234 of 256 candidates were assembled and then discarded by the biome check at javaStructureStarts.ts:225, consuming 87% of all layout time. Moving the check in front of the builder takes the front model from 310 ms per chunk of layout to about 40, and the fill model from 9.4 to about 1.2. Memoizing jigsawBlocksOf, which is 20.9% self time with no cache, is the same saving taken from the other side.

This was written up as the one item here that deviates from Java's order, and it is the opposite. The jar settled it. Structure.generate calls findValidGenerationPoint, which is findGenerationPoint(context).filter(context::isValidBiome), and findGenerationPoint returns a GenerationStub holding a position and an Either<Consumer<StructurePiecesBuilder>, StructurePiecesBuilder>. Thirteen of the fourteen structure types this server builds answer with the Consumer side, which has not run; getPiecesBuilder() runs it on the line after the filter. JigsawPlacement.addPieces, which is the expensive one, places only the start piece and defers the breadth-first walk into that consumer. Java checks the biome before it assembles. Our order was the deviation, and putting the check first ends it rather than introducing one.

The one exception is the mineshaft, and Java agrees there too: MineshaftStructure.findGenerationPoint answers with Either.right of a built pieces list, because the settling move the tunnels make is what decides its own generation point. It stays eager.

Measured at seed 1234 over the same 102 by 102 area: 356 candidates before and after, 47 assembled where 356 were, and the 37 kept starts identical in structure, set, chunk, step, terrain adaptation, generation point, bounding box and piece count. The generation golden corpus did not move.

The pool buys 4.7 times and no more. That is measured, twice, on this machine, and it is a property of six performance cores and V8 isolates rather than of this code. More workers past six made it worse. It is also the point at which terrain becomes roughly 90% of the per-chunk cost and everything else stops mattering.

The terrain fill is 227 ms for 98304 blocks, 2.3 microseconds per block, and after the two rows above it is the whole remaining problem. The density stack is already in good shape: density/compile.ts compiles the function graph to closures, and SampleContext.CornerCache is a flat direct-mapped Float64Array lattice rather than deepslate's template-string Map, which is worth 36 times on corner lookups by itself. What is left is the per-block work: one final_density evaluation and one Aquifer.computeSubstance for every block of the column, from the top of the world to the bottom, with no early exit and one closure call per node. The 2.5 times row is a target, not a measurement.

The conclusion this was taken on. 50 chunks per second needs the ladder, the structure rejection fix and the pool together, and then a terrain fill several times faster than the one measured. No stage reaches the target alone, and the pool, which is the part that sounds like the answer, was expected to be worth less than the two algorithmic stages beside it.

That rests on terrain being 227 ms per chunk and about 90% of the per-chunk cost once the ladder and the rejection fix have landed. Both halves of that turned out to be wrong, which is the subject of the next section.

The terrain rows are an average and the beardifier is why they are not a constant. A chunk away from every structure pays 0.5 ms for it; a chunk inside an ancient city's beard pays 220 ms per terrain tile. The ladder takes that from three tiles per chunk to one, which is the largest single thing the ladder buys that is not in the 454 ms, and indexing the pieces by column instead of scanning the list on every sample takes most of the rest. Until that lands, terrain near a large structure costs several times the 227 ms these rows assume, in exactly the places players walk to.

Terrain is 30% of a chunk, not 90%. The decoration pass and the Bedrock translation are 161 of the 239 ms. The structure layout, which the section above treats as the expensive half of the problem, is seven milliseconds once a candidate is rejected before it is assembled. The projections assumed the ladder would leave terrain as nearly all of what remained; it left features as nearly all of it.

That is why a pool over terrain alone was worth 1.29 times. It is what the split predicts: one status of the three left the server thread, so about 70% of a chunk stayed serial and 1/(0.7 + 0.3/6) is 1.33. The pool was not undersized or misconfigured. It was pooling the part that was not the cost.

So the whole ladder runs in the worker. Only the Bedrock translation stays on the server thread, because it reads the Java-to-Bedrock tables and is a palette lookup rather than a per-block walk. Java keeps features on a single executor because Java writes a feature through into its neighbors; this server's decoration pass runs over a private view and returns patches, which is what makes it safe to run anywhere. ShardedGenerationPool then gives each worker a region of the world so the neighbors a chunk reads are ones that worker has already built.

WebAssembly was built and reverted. A terrain kernel was written, measured bit-identical and merged, and then taken out again: 6.4 times on the arithmetic applies to 30% of a chunk, so the ceiling it was reaching for was about 1.25 times overall. The cost was a second implementation of the density graph, the noise and the aquifer, each of which had to stay bit-identical to the JavaScript beside it forever. The measurements that justified it are still in parallel-chunk-generation-in-node.md; what was wrong was not the 6.4 times, it was which fraction of the chunk it applied to.

What guarantees byte-identical output ​

This is the project's parity claim and it is the thing most easily lost, so each guarantee is stated with the mechanism that enforces it rather than as an intention.

1. Every status body is a pure function of the seed, the chunk position and the frozen outputs of lower statuses. The step table declares, per status, the status each neighbor at each distance is guaranteed to have reached. The proto chunk view a status is handed refuses a read outside that window and throws, the way WorldGenRegion.getChunk builds a CrashReport rather than returning a chunk at the wrong status. An undeclared input is a crash on the first chunk, not a silent difference on the thousandth.

2. Each (chunk, status) body runs exactly once. The holder keeps one promise per status, the first caller creates it and every other caller awaits it. Nothing re-enters a status, so no step can be applied twice with different inputs. Only the thread that owns the holder map mutates it.

3. Every random draw is reseeded from the seed, a position and a fixed index. That is Java's rule and packages/features/src/placement/WorldgenRandom.ts already implements setDecorationSeed, setFeatureSeed and setLargeFeatureSeed, and packages/worldgen/src/random/ the two random sources they run over. The fixed index is the global feature index from sortFeaturesPerStep, which depends on the registry contents and on nothing else. Three things would break this and all three are mechanical: Math.random is seeded independently per V8 isolate and produced seven different sequences across a main thread and six workers; localeCompare orders identifiers differently from a plain comparison; and an object whose keys are numeric strings enumerates them before the rest. A lint rule bans the first two under the generation packages, and ordered data uses Map rather than a plain object.

4. No two concurrent bodies write the same block. Every status except features writes only its own chunk. features writes radius 1, and its writes outside its own chunk are recorded as a patch rather than applied, so two feature passes that overlap never race. Each produces an independent patch, and the owning holder merges them. The merge order is fixed by position, the chunk's own writes first and then the eight inbound patches in a fixed neighbor order, so the merged result is a function of position alone.

5. Float arithmetic is identical across isolates. Measured: transcendentals, a*b+c without FMA contraction, object, Map and Set iteration order and Array.prototype.sort were bit-identical across a main thread and six workers, and identical again under --no-opt, --jitless and --max-opt=0. 36 chunk digests matched across 1, 2, 4, 6, 8 and 10 workers. The one way to lose this is to rewrite a kernel in another language and reassociate an expression. The measured WebAssembly port did exactly that and diverged by 1.4e-13, so a port carries a bit-pattern fixture test per expression, not a tolerance.

Together those make the output of every status a function of the seed and the position. The falsifiable form of that claim, which the parity corpus runs, is that the digests of a fixed set of chunks are identical across generation orders (row major, reversed, and a seeded shuffle) and across pool sizes (inline, 1, 2, 4, 6, 8).

The one divergence from Java we accept ​

Java's feature pass writes through a live WorldGenRegion straight into the neighbor's ProtoChunk, so a tree in chunk C can see and be blocked by a tree that chunk N's own feature pass already placed. Whether it does depends on which of C and N reached features first, which depends on scheduling. Java's own output at a chunk border is therefore not a function of the seed alone.

Recording a patch instead of writing through makes our output a function of the seed alone, at the price of one case: a feature whose placement decision reads a block that a neighboring chunk's own feature pass wrote. That feature now reads the neighbor's terrain and does not see the neighbor's features. The failure mode is two features from different chunks occupying the same space instead of the second being refused.

This is not a new divergence. DataDrivenGenerator.generateChunk already reads neighbors through copyCenterFrom over radius-0 terrain regions, so the current generator has the same read semantics; what changes is only that the writes stop being replayed into already-emitted chunks. The size of the affected set is measurable: the same chunk generated first and generated last differs at 169 of 98304 positions today, 0.17%, and that was measured before the biome filter fix cut the feature count per chunk.

The alternative, a positional total order over feature passes, was rejected on cost rather than on principle. Any such order needs a proper colouring of the conflict graph, two chunks conflict when their Chebyshev distance is 2 or less, and the chromatic number of that graph is nine. A nine-deep colour chain means one cold chunk drags a 33 by 33 neighborhood of feature passes with it before it can finish, which is a stall on every teleport into ungenerated terrain.

What it cost in the step table. One row. Java's full step declares no dependency at distance 1, because the feature pass has already written through and there is nothing left to collect; ours reads its eight neighbors at features to take their patches, so it declares that and the read is checked like every other. The accumulated table does not move, because full already guaranteed initialize_light at distance 1 and initialize_light is above features. Every radius, RADIUS_AROUND_FULL_CHUNK and SAFETY_MARGIN_CHUNKS read what they read before.

What it cost at the border, measured. Over the generation corpus, 1.4% to 1.9% of a decorated chunk's positions, and the whole of it in one direction: a chunk gains blocks its neighbors' feature passes owe it. None of it is a block changing its mind. The 169-position figure above sizes something narrower, the order-dependent set, and it is not the size of the one-time move.

Alternatives rejected ​

Keep the 3 by 3 region and add workers. 43% of the per-chunk cost is the window and the patch-back is order-dependent, so this parallelizes the waste and turns a reproducible defect into a scheduling-dependent one. The patch-back also writes straight into an already-emitted Chunk with setBlockRuntimeId, bypassing World.writeBlockRuntimeId, so a chunk already sent to a player silently changes.

Copy Java's threading exactly: features inline on one single-task executor. This keeps Java's read semantics and pays for them twice. The output stops being a function of the seed, so the order-independence check cannot be green; and the one remaining serial step caps throughput at one over its own cost, which at today's 16 ms is 62 chunks per second whatever the pool does.

Generate 4 by 4 or 16 by 16 chunk regions per worker. It amortizes the shared per-column work and sends 16 chunks in one message. The message cost it saves is already 0.2% of a core at 100 chunks per second, and it makes the unit of work something other than the chunk the server asks for. Worth revisiting if per-column amortization turns out to be large; not worth paying for the messaging.

A separate generation process over a socket. Same event loop isolation as workers, plus crash isolation this code does not need, and it loses both transferable ArrayBuffer and the shared registry bundle. The bundle was the largest measured startup saving: 75 ms for eight workers against 540 ms reading from disk and 289 ms through workerData, where the workerData cost lands on the main thread.

A WebAssembly terrain kernel. Built, measured bit-identical, merged and then reverted. See what it measured once it was built: 6.4 times on the arithmetic is 6.4 times on 30% of a chunk, and the price was a second implementation of the density graph, the noise and the aquifer that had to stay bit-identical forever.

A native addon instead of WebAssembly. It buys SIMD and real shared memory and costs a compiled artifact per platform. These packages are published to npm, so a mandatory native addon changes every consumer's install. It also lets the compiler contract multiply-add into a fused instruction unless told not to, which is a world that is subtly not Java's and would pass every tolerance-based test. Moot now for the same reason the WebAssembly kernel is.

deepslate. Already rejected in packages/worldgen/README.md. It has no carvers and no placed features, and it is slower than this repository's own sampler: 630 ms for a terrain pass against 227 ms for a whole terrain-only chunk here, because it looks cell corners up in a Map keyed by a template string at 108 ns against 3 ns for a flat Float64Array lattice.

Consequences ​

Generator.generateChunk(cx, cz, seed, config): Promise<Chunk> keeps its signature, so Anvil.readChunk, LevelDB.readChunk and ChunkScheduler are unaffected. DataDrivenGenerator becomes an adapter that asks the generation service for a chunk at full.

ChunkScheduler.tick starts deliveries within its 5 ms per-tick budget and returns without waiting for generation. It keeps up to the host's Overworld worker count in flight, capped at six, so player requests use the same pool as the start-region preload. Completed chunks are flushed on a later tick, with each player's batches sent in order. This lets the server keep ticking while workers build chunks. World.preload continues to batch 32 requests through Promise.all (World.ts:759).

A request carries how badly its chunk is wanted, because a pool with a backlog has to be told what to serve first. A chunk a player is standing next to is immediate, one inside half their view distance is soon, and the rim beyond it and the whole start region preload are idle.

applyFeaturePatch, applyPatchToChunk, copyCenterFrom, featurePatches, generatedChunks and serializeGeneration are deleted. GENERATION_CACHE_SIZE goes with them. Holders are retained by the generation task that needs them and released when every chunk in their write radius has reached full, which replaces an LRU that silently dropped cross-chunk writes past 128 chunks.

Chunk border content changes once, at the slice that lands the patch merge. The golden corpus is re-captured in that commit and the order-independence check goes green there.

The server gains a thread pool with a lifecycle. It is built at world load, pinned to one seed per worker because RandomState costs 55 to 134 ms to rebuild, and terminated on shutdown. worker.terminate() interrupts a busy worker in 2 to 24 ms, which is the only way to stop one, because worker threads do not receive signals.

Memory grows with the retained working set. Six workers each holding the registries cost about 80 MiB apiece on top of the shared bytes, roughly 500 MiB, which is fine on 16 GiB and needs rechecking on a small VPS.
